\begin{abstract}
Pretrained spatio-temporal foundation models such as OpenCity achieve strong zero-shot traffic forecasting, but performance degrades under cross-city distribution shifts.
In NLP and time-series domains, in-context learning (ICL) allows models to adapt predictions using retrieved demonstrations at inference time, yet ICL remains unexplored for spatio-temporal foundation models.
The standard ICL approach of feeding demonstrations into the model's context window cannot be directly applied: spatio-temporal foundation models are typically pretrained on fixed-length sequences, and extending sequences with demonstrations produces out-of-distribution attention patterns that degrade performance by 9--28\%.
We propose a lightweight, pluggable ICL module that keeps the base model completely frozen.
A small aggregation network (198K parameters) is trained offline in seconds from cached features; at inference time, KNN retrieves the most similar training examples, and the aggregator combines their prediction errors into a query-specific correction without any test-time weight updates.
On PEMS07M, our method reduces MAE from 4.50 to 4.29 (+4.7\%) with only 0.8\% additional parameters, approaching full-shot supervised baselines while the backbone remains entirely frozen.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Accurate traffic forecasting supports urban applications such as route planning and signal control.
Traditional spatio-temporal models such as STGCN~\cite{yu2018stgcn} and Graph WaveNet~\cite{wu2019graphwavenet} achieve strong supervised performance but require per-dataset training with target-domain labels.
OpenCity~\cite{li2024opencity} represents a new paradigm: a spatio-temporal foundation model (ST~FM) pretrained on large-scale heterogeneous traffic data, achieving zero-shot transfer across cities, data categories, and time horizons through an architecture integrating Transformers with graph neural networks.
However, despite strong zero-shot capability, performance degrades under larger cross-city distribution shifts.
For example, on the SZ-DIDI and CD-DIDI datasets, OpenCity's zero-shot MAE falls noticeably behind full-shot supervised baselines, exposing the limits of purely static zero-shot inference.
OpenCity addresses this through gradient-based fine-tuning of its prediction head (3~epochs on target-domain data), which improves performance but raises the question: can demonstration-based adaptation at inference time complement or rival fine-tuning for ST~foundation models?

In NLP, in-context learning (ICL) offers this capability, allowing foundation models to adapt by conditioning on demonstrations without gradient updates~\cite{brown2020gpt3}.
Chen~et~al.~\cite{chen2022ict} formalize ICL as a meta-learning objective through In-Context Tuning (ICT), training language models to predict target labels given concatenated instruction, in-context examples, and query.
ICT bridges prompting, fine-tuning, and meta-learning into a unified framework, outperforming gradient-based meta-learning such as MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} by leveraging the language model's pattern-matching inductive bias.
Das~et~al.~\cite{das2024icf} extend ICL to time-series foundation models through In-Context Fine-Tuning (ICF), adapting TimesFM~\cite{das2024timesfm} to use related time series in its context window via separator tokens and cross-example attention.
TimesFM-ICF achieves 7--25\% improvement over the base model, even rivaling per-dataset fine-tuning.
These results show that providing related examples at inference time helps models adapt, not only in language but also in numerical forecasting.
Yet ICL remains unexplored for spatio-temporal foundation models.

Both ICT and TimesFM-ICF enable ICL by modifying the model, either through fine-tuning or continued pretraining with architectural changes.
For spatio-temporal foundation models, such modification is non-trivial.
ST~FMs are typically pretrained on fixed-length input sequences with specific attention patterns and positional encodings; OpenCity, for instance, uses 24~patches (288~timesteps).
Naively extending input sequences to include demonstrations causes out-of-distribution self-attention patterns, positional encoding mismatches, and temporal-categorical cross-attention failures.
Our preliminary experiments confirm this: naive sequence extension degrades MAE by 9--28\% versus zero-shot (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
Enabling native ICL would require architectural modification and continued pretraining, at high cost and with the risk of degrading pretrained representations.
This motivates an approach that never extends the model's input sequence: process query and demonstrations independently through the standard forward path, and combine their information only in output space.

We propose \textbf{ICL-Traffic}, a residual correction framework for enabling demonstration-based adaptation in frozen ST~FMs.
Each demonstration's prediction error estimates the model's bias on similar inputs; a learned aggregation network combines these errors into a query-specific correction.
Two axes drive improvement: (1)~KNN retrieval ensures each query receives relevant demonstrations from a training-set demo pool, and (2)~a learned aggregator (8K--198K parameters) replaces naive averaging with query-aware weighting.
The base model remains completely frozen; the aggregator is trained offline in seconds from cached features.
Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We are the first to bring in-context learning to spatio-temporal foundation models. Inspired by ICT~\cite{chen2022ict} and TimesFM-ICF~\cite{das2024icf}, we propose a residual correction framework that enables demonstration-based adaptation for frozen ST~FMs without architectural modification or base model retraining.

\item We systematically study two axes of the framework---demonstration retrieval (random vs.\ KNN) and correction aggregation (naive average vs.\ cosine similarity vs.\ cross-attention)---showing both are critical and jointly necessary to surpass zero-shot performance.

\item On PEMS07M with OpenCity-plus, our best configuration achieves +4.7\% MAE improvement (4.50$\to$4.29) with only 198K external parameters (${\sim}$0.8\% of the base model). We further plan a direct comparison against OpenCity's 3-epoch fast adaptation on SZ-DIDI and CD-DIDI under the same training budget.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Foundation Models}

Traditional spatio-temporal models such as STGCN~\cite{yu2018stgcn}, Graph WaveNet~\cite{wu2019graphwavenet}, AGCRN~\cite{bai2020agcrn}, and ASTGCN~\cite{guo2019astgcn} achieve strong supervised performance but require per-dataset training with target-domain labels.
Recent foundation models address this through large-scale pretraining across multiple cities and data types.

OpenCity~\cite{li2024opencity}, our base model, integrates Transformer architecture with graph neural networks.
It employs instance normalization for distribution shift handling, patch embedding for long-horizon prediction, and temporal-categorical joint attention.
Pretrained on heterogeneous multi-city traffic data covering flow, speed, and demand categories, OpenCity achieves zero-shot performance competitive with full-shot baselines across six benchmark datasets and demonstrates promising scaling laws.
When zero-shot performance is insufficient, OpenCity provides fast adaptation via 3-epoch fine-tuning of its prediction head on target-domain data.
However, this remains gradient-based adaptation; in-context learning has not been explored for OpenCity or any other ST~FM.

Other ST~FMs include UniST~\cite{yuan2024unist}, which uses prompt-based universal prediction; UrbanGPT~\cite{li2024urbangpt}, which integrates spatio-temporal prediction with a large language model backbone; and FlashST~\cite{li2024flashst}, which uses prompt-tuning for fast adaptation.
All adapt via fine-tuning or prompt-tuning; none explores ICL.

\subsection{In-Context Learning}

GPT-3~\cite{brown2020gpt3} first showed that large language models can adapt to new tasks through few-shot demonstrations in the input context, without gradient updates.

Chen~et~al.~\cite{chen2022ict} formalize this through In-Context Tuning (ICT).
ICT recasts few-shot learning as sequence prediction: given concatenated instruction, labeled in-context examples, and a target query, the model is trained to predict the target label.
By meta-training across diverse NLP tasks, ICT teaches language models to \textit{learn from demonstrations} as a general capability.
ICT outperforms gradient-based meta-learning (MAML~\cite{finn2017maml}) by leveraging the language model's inductive bias for pattern matching, and reduces sensitivity to example ordering (6$\times$) and selection (2$\times$).
The core insight relevant to our work is that models can be trained to extract useful patterns from contextual examples and apply them to novel queries.

Das~et~al.~\cite{das2024icf} extend ICL to time-series foundation models through In-Context Fine-Tuning (ICF).
Starting from TimesFM~\cite{das2024timesfm}, they introduce separator tokens between in-context examples, enable cross-example causal attention, and remove positional encoding for length generalization.
Through continued pretraining, the model learns to borrow patterns from related time series in its context window.
TimesFM-ICF achieves 7--25\% improvement over the base model and even surpasses per-dataset fine-tuning on the Monash benchmark, showing that ICL extends beyond language to numerical forecasting.
However, TimesFM-ICF requires both architectural modification and continued pretraining.
We share the goal of leveraging related examples for inference-time adaptation, but pursue a complementary approach: keep the ST~model completely frozen and achieve adaptation through output-space residual correction.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Consider a traffic network represented as a graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathbf{A})$ with $N = |\mathcal{V}|$ sensor nodes and adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$.
Given historical observations $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N \times F}$ over $T$ time steps with $F$ features per node, the goal is to predict future values $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times N}$ over the next $T'$ steps.

Let $f_\theta$ denote a frozen pretrained ST~foundation model with parameters $\theta$, producing predictions $\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = f_\theta(\mathbf{X})$.
We assume access to a demonstration pool $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{X}_d, \mathbf{Y}_d)\}_{d=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}$ from the target-domain training set, where ground truth $\mathbf{Y}_d$ is available.
Given a test query $\mathbf{X}_q$, our goal is to select $K$ demonstrations from $\mathcal{D}$ and produce an improved prediction $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_q$ that surpasses $f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_q)$, without modifying $\theta$.

\subsection{Residual Correction Framework}

We estimate and correct the frozen model's prediction error using demonstrations.
For each selected demonstration $(\mathbf{X}_k, \mathbf{Y}_k)$, we compute the residual correction:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_k = \mathbf{Y}_k - f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_k)
\end{equation}
which captures the model's bias on input $\mathbf{X}_k$.
The corrected prediction for query $\mathbf{X}_q$ is:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_q = f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_q) + \mathrm{Aggregate}\!\left(\{\mathbf{c}_k\}_{k=1}^K\right)
\label{eq:correction}
\end{equation}

This framework has several desirable properties.
First, every input is processed through the model's standard forward path with no sequence extension, eliminating out-of-distribution risk.
Second, when $K = 0$, the framework degenerates to the original zero-shot prediction.
Third, when corrections are uncorrelated noise (i.e., random demonstrations), the expected aggregate converges to zero as sampling rounds increase, recovering zero-shot performance---a property we verify experimentally.

Like ICT~\cite{chen2022ict} and TimesFM-ICF~\cite{das2024icf}, our method uses demonstrations to adapt predictions at inference time, but we operate entirely in output space without modifying the base model.

\subsection{Demonstration Retrieval}

The quality of residual corrections depends critically on the similarity between demonstrations and the query.
We investigate two retrieval strategies.

\textbf{Random selection} samples $K$ demonstrations uniformly from $\mathcal{D}$.
Because randomly selected demos have low correlation with the query, their corrections are effectively noise.
With $K=3$, random selection yields MAE~6.14, which is 36\% worse than zero-shot, confirming that demo quality matters.

\textbf{Raw-input KNN} retrieves the $K$ nearest neighbors based on L2 distance in the flattened input space:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_K(\mathbf{X}_q) = \underset{\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D},\, |\mathcal{S}|=K}{\arg\min} \sum_{d \in \mathcal{S}} \|\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X}_q) - \mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X}_d)\|_2
\end{equation}
This simple approach dramatically improves over random selection (MAE 6.14$\to$4.66 for $K=3$).
KNN retrieval is deterministic, meaning multiple sampling rounds $S$ have no effect, which confirms that demo-query alignment is the key factor rather than averaging over more samples.

A planned extension uses \textbf{embedding-space KNN}, computing distances in the frozen encoder's feature space (node-mean pooled, 512~dimensions), which should better capture semantic similarity.

\subsection{Learned Aggregation}

Naive uniform averaging $(1/K)\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf{c}_k$ dilutes informative corrections with residual noise, failing to surpass zero-shot performance.
We replace it with a learned aggregation module:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{C}} = \mathrm{Aggregator}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_q,\; \{\mathbf{h}_k\}_{k=1}^K,\; \{\mathbf{c}_k\}_{k=1}^K\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_q$ and $\mathbf{h}_k$ are encoder features extracted from the frozen model.
We study two architectures.

\subsubsection{SimpleDemoAggregator (${\sim}$8K parameters)}
Query and demonstration encoder features are projected to a shared low-dimensional space.
Cosine similarity between the query projection and each demo projection produces attention scores, which are normalized via temperature-scaled softmax to yield per-demo weights.
The final correction is a weighted sum of $\{\mathbf{c}_k\}$.
This design gives interpretable weights and achieves MAE~4.47.

\subsubsection{Cross-Attention Aggregator (${\sim}$198K parameters)}
Multi-head cross-attention is applied with the query features as queries and demonstration features as keys and values.
Each attention head produces a weighted combination of corrections.
A learned head-combine layer merges the per-head outputs, and an adaptive output gate (zero-initialized softplus for training stability) scales the final correction.
This architecture captures richer query-demo interactions and achieves MAE~4.29--4.30.

\subsubsection{Training Procedure}
Training proceeds in two phases.
\textbf{Phase~1} (one-time, ${\sim}$3h GPU): the frozen base model runs forward passes over all training windows, precomputing and caching predictions $f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_d)$, encoder features $\mathbf{h}_d$, and corrections $\mathbf{c}_d = \mathbf{Y}_d - f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_d)$ for every $d \in \mathcal{D}$.
\textbf{Phase~2} (aggregator training, ${\sim}$5 seconds): the aggregator is trained from cached data only, with no forward or backward pass through the base model, using Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and MAE loss.
The base model is used only once during Phase~1; all subsequent training and inference operate on cached features.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Dataset.}
PEMS07M contains 228 sensor nodes recording highway traffic speed at 5-minute intervals.
We use a 50/10/40 train/validation/test split, yielding a demonstration pool of 5,761 training windows.

\textbf{Base model.}
OpenCity-plus~\cite{li2024opencity} with embedding dimension 512, encoder depth 6, 16 attention heads, and ${\sim}$26M parameters.
PEMS07M is unseen during OpenCity's pretraining, making this a genuine zero-shot transfer setting.

\textbf{Metrics.}
Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE, \%), and Pearson Correlation (CORR).

\textbf{Implementation.}
PyTorch 2.4.1 on NVIDIA L40S (46GB), fp32 precision.

\subsection{Main Results}

Table~\ref{tab:main} presents our main results on PEMS07M.
The progression tells a clear story.
First, random demonstrations severely degrade performance (MAE 4.50$\to$6.14, $-$36\%), confirming that uncorrelated corrections introduce harmful noise.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\textbf{Why random demonstrations hurt.}
Random corrections are uncorrelated with the query's prediction error.
Averaging such noise introduces systematic distortion.
As the number of sampling rounds $S \to \infty$, the average correction converges to the population mean error across all training windows, effectively recovering zero-shot performance.
This confirms that random demonstrations carry no query-specific signal.

\textbf{Why KNN is necessary but not sufficient.}
Similar inputs yield correlated prediction errors, making KNN-retrieved corrections informative.
However, naive uniform averaging dilutes the signal: even among the $K$ nearest neighbors, residual noise remains.
Learned weighting is needed to focus on the most relevant corrections and suppress outliers.

\textbf{Why learned aggregation crosses the threshold.}
The aggregator learns query-aware weights that focus on the most relevant corrections.
Even the SimpleDemoAggregator (8K~parameters) surpasses zero-shot, confirming that learned weighting improves over uniform averaging.
The Cross-Attention Aggregator captures richer interactions through multi-head attention, yielding the largest improvement.

\textbf{Convergence.}
The aggregator converges by epoch~3--4 from cached features (${\sim}$5~seconds total).
The 3-epoch result (MAE~4.30) nearly matches 10-epoch (4.29), confirming rapid convergence and providing a fair-comparison epoch budget with OpenCity's fast adaptation.

\textbf{Failed approach: naive sequence extension.}
We initially attempted standard ICL by concatenating demonstrations into the model's input sequence.
This caused out-of-distribution self-attention patterns and positional encoding mismatches, degrading MAE by 9--28\% versus zero-shot.
This failure motivates our output-space residual correction approach.

\textbf{Per-query retrieval vs.\ fine-tuning.}
Fine-tuning aggregates gradient signals from all training examples equally into weight updates, producing a globally adapted model.
Our method allows training examples highly similar to a specific test query to exert disproportionate influence through KNN retrieval and learned aggregation.
This per-query adaptation may be particularly beneficial under distribution shifts where the demo pool contains local structure that a global weight update would dilute.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Compute overhead.}
Our method requires $K{+}1$ forward passes per test sample (4$\times$ for $K{=}3$).
This is partially offset by the fact that demonstration forward passes can be precomputed and cached during Phase~1.

\textbf{Output-space correction, not true ICL.}
Demonstrations do not enter the model's attention context; corrections are applied in output space only.
This is a practical constraint of the fixed-length pretraining of current ST~FMs.
True ICL, where the model attends over demonstrations and query jointly as in ICT~\cite{chen2022ict} and TimesFM-ICF~\cite{das2024icf}, would require architectural modification and continued pretraining.

\textbf{Trainable parameters.}
Our aggregation module requires an offline training phase (${\sim}$5~seconds from cached features).
Like fine-tuning, we use target-domain training data.
The difference is in how data is used: fine-tuning absorbs training data into model weights, producing a single globally adapted model; our method preserves training data as a retrievable demo pool, and the aggregator learns a general correction weighting strategy.
At inference time, per-query variation comes from demo retrieval, not from the aggregator weights (which are fixed for all queries).

\textbf{Retrieval ceiling.}
Raw-input L2 KNN may miss semantic similarity in high-dimensional feature space.
Embedding-space KNN using the frozen encoder's representations should improve retrieval quality.

\subsection{Planned Experiments}

Several extensions are planned to strengthen the empirical evaluation.
\textbf{Embedding-space KNN}: using frozen encoder features (node-mean pooling, 512~dimensions) for retrieval, expected to improve over raw-input L2 distance.
\textbf{K sweep}: evaluating $K \in \{1, 3, 5\}$ for both naive averaging and Cross-Attention aggregation.
\textbf{Multi-dataset validation}: experiments on SZ-DIDI and CD-DIDI for a direct comparison against OpenCity's fast adaptation (3-epoch prediction head fine-tuning) under the same training budget but a different adaptation mechanism.
\textbf{Detailed analysis}: per-hour MAE curves, attention weight visualization, and per-node improvement maps.

In the longer term, the goal is a spatio-temporal foundation model natively capable of ICL, analogous to TimesFM-ICF~\cite{das2024icf}, which would require continued pretraining with cross-example attention.
Our output-space correction provides initial evidence for this direction.

\section{Conclusion}

We are the first to bring in-context learning to spatio-temporal foundation models.
Inspired by In-Context Tuning~\cite{chen2022ict} and TimesFM-ICF~\cite{das2024icf}, we propose a residual correction framework that enables demonstration-based adaptation for frozen ST~FMs without architectural modification.
Our key findings: (1)~demonstration retrieval quality is the dominant factor, as random demonstrations hurt while KNN retrieval is necessary but not sufficient alone; (2)~learned aggregation via cross-attention crosses the zero-shot threshold, confirming that both retrieval and aggregation are jointly needed; (3)~the best configuration achieves +4.7\% MAE improvement with only 198K parameters (${\sim}$0.8\% of the base model), approaching full-shot supervised baselines.
Our method differs from test-time adaptation (no test-time gradient updates) and complements fine-tuning (same training data, different usage).
Within the broader ICL progression---ICT trains models for ICL, TimesFM-ICF enables ICL via continued pretraining---our work shows that output-space ICL through demo retrieval and correction works even with a frozen model.
